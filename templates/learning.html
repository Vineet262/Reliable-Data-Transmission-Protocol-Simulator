Reliable Data Transmission

Underlying physical channels (IP layer) are inherently unreliable: packets may be corrupted, delayed, reordered, or lost. Reliable data transfer guarantees that the data received is exactly what was transmitted, with zero loss, zero corruption, and in exact order.

Service: rdt_send() → [Unreliable Net] → rdt_rcv()

Sequence Numbers & Packets

Every packet carries a sequence number in its header. This allows the receiver to detect duplicate frames caused by retransmissions and reconstruct out-of-order data in chronological order.

[ Header: Seq #k | Payload: Data bytes ]

Acknowledgements (ACKs)

Positive control packets sent back by the receiver informing the sender that data packets have arrived intact. Cumulative ACKs confirm all packets up to sequence k, while Individual ACKs confirm only packet k.

Receiver → ACK(k) → Sender

Timeouts & Retransmission

If a packet or its corresponding ACK is lost, the sender's countdown timer expires. The sender triggers retransmission of the packet. The timeout must safely exceed the Round-Trip Time (RTT) to prevent premature retransmissions.

Timeout Threshold > 2 × One-Way Propagation Delay

Sliding Window Concept

Allows the sender to transmit up to W packets before requiring an acknowledgement. As acknowledgements arrive, the window slides forward over the sequence space, maintaining high pipeline utilization.

Window: [base, base + W - 1]

Performance Metrics

Throughput: Unique payload bits delivered per second.
Delivery Ratio: (Delivered packets / Total packets) × 100%.
Efficiency: (Unique delivered bits / Total transmitted bits) × 100%.

Throughput = (Unique Bits) / Elapsed Seconds

Stop-and-Wait ARQ Walkthrough

Sender                  Receiver
  |                        |
  |--- Packet 0 ---------->| (Accepted)
  |<-- ACK 0 --------------| (Acked)
  |                        |
  |--- Packet 1 ---------->| (DROPPED!)
  |    [Timer Expires]     |
  |--- Packet 1 (Retx) --->| (Accepted)
  |<-- ACK 1 --------------|
  |                        |

How it works: The sender transmits packet 0 and starts a timer. When ACK 0 is received, it cancels the timer and sends packet 1. If packet 1 is lost, the sender's timer expires and retransmits packet 1. Simple, but channel capacity remains idle during the entire round-trip delay.

Go-Back-N ARQ Walkthrough

Sender (W=4)            Receiver
  |                        |
  |--- Packet 0 ---------->| (Accepted)
  |--- Packet 1 ---------->| (DROPPED!)
  |--- Packet 2 ---------->| (Discarded: Expected 1)
  |--- Packet 3 ---------->| (Discarded: Expected 1)
  |<-- ACK 0 --------------| (Window slides to 1)
  |    [Timeout for 1]     |
  |--- Packet 1 (Retx) --->| (Retransmits all
  |--- Packet 2 (Retx) --->|  outstanding packets
  |--- Packet 3 (Retx) --->|  1, 2, 3)

How it works: Sender transmits up to W packets. The receiver accepts packets in strict sequential order. If packet 1 is dropped, subsequent packets (2, 3) are discarded. When the timer for packet 1 expires, the sender goes back and retransmits packets 1, 2, and 3.

Selective Repeat ARQ Walkthrough

Sender (W=4)            Receiver
  |                        |
  |--- Packet 0 ---------->| (Accepted & Delivered)
  |--- Packet 1 ---------->| (DROPPED!)
  |--- Packet 2 ---------->| (Buffered in RX buffer)
  |--- Packet 3 ---------->| (Buffered in RX buffer)
  |<-- ACK 0 --------------|
  |<-- ACK 2 --------------| (Marked acked in TX)
  |<-- ACK 3 --------------| (Marked acked in TX)
  |    [Timeout for 1]     |
  |--- Packet 1 (Retx) --->| (Received & Delivers 1,2,3!)

How it works: Receiver has an out-of-order buffer. Packets 2 and 3 are buffered and acknowledged individually. Only packet 1 times out. The sender retransmits ONLY packet 1. Once packet 1 arrives, packets 1, 2, and 3 are delivered in sequence to the application.

Question 1 Sliding Windows

Why does Stop-and-Wait achieve significantly lower throughput than Go-Back-N over a high-bandwidth-delay product link?

Answer: In Stop-and-Wait, the sender must keep the channel completely idle during the Round-Trip Time (RTT) waiting for the ACK of each single packet. In Go-Back-N, the sender pipelines up to W packets back-to-back, filling the transmission pipe and maximizing channel utilization.

Question 2 Acknowledgements

In Go-Back-N, if packets 0, 1, and 2 are sent, and ACK 0 and ACK 1 are lost in the network, but ACK 2 arrives safely, does the sender need to retransmit packets 0 and 1?

Answer: No. Because Go-Back-N employs cumulative acknowledgements, ACK 2 implicitly confirms the successful arrival of all packets up to and including sequence 2. The lost ACKs 0 and 1 are harmlessly superseded.

Question 3 Selective Repeat

What is the primary architectural trade-off that makes Selective Repeat more complex to implement than Go-Back-N?

Answer: Selective Repeat requires memory buffering at the receiver to store valid out-of-order packets until missing frames arrive, as well as multiple independent hardware/software timers at the sender for each packet in flight.

Question 4 Window Constraints

For a Selective Repeat protocol with sequence numbers of k bits (sequence space $2^k$), what is the maximum allowable window size $W$, and why?

Answer: $W \le 2^{k-1}$ (half the sequence space). If $W > 2^{k-1}$, an overlapping receiver window could confuse a retransmitted packet from the previous cycle with a brand new packet from the subsequent sequence cycle upon ACK loss.
